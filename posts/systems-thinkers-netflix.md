Elizabeth Stone, Netflix's Chief Product and Technology Officer, argues that AI has scrambled the boundaries of every role in tech. PMs can ship code, designers can write specs, engineers can shape product decisions — and the result, she says, is a confusing "storming phase" before a new, more fluid way of working settles in. Her point isn't that specialization is dead: she still finds great engineering, design, and data science scarce, and believes deep craft remains essential wherever it exists. What's fading is narrow, static specialization — people who only know one lane and don't grow beyond it.

The skill Stone says is rising fastest, across every function, is systems thinking: the ability to step back from the task in front of you and ask what you're assuming about the larger problem it sits inside. Her practical version of this is simple — before building anything, take "one click" outward and check whether you're solving the right problem, not just solving a problem well. Netflix is hiring more people who can architect at that level (shared infrastructure, design systems, platforms that let hundreds of people move fast without reinventing basics), and treating AI fluency as a baseline expectation at every level of the career ladder, not a specialized skill tied to a particular title.

Underneath all of it is what Stone calls "excellence as an operating system" — Netflix's long-standing bet on talent density, high autonomy, and tolerance for risk instead of leaning on heavy process to catch mistakes. Her broader argument is that AI doesn't replace the need for judgment and craft; it raises the value of people who can reason about how the pieces of a system fit together, because the work itself is increasingly done by both humans and AI agents acting together, and someone still has to be accountable for whether the whole thing actually works.
